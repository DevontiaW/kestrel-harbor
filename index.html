Observability: seeing inside the agent work

Where is the agent work waiting right now?

The AI (artificial intelligence) agents here look busy, yet work still piles up. To see where, every task writes a line in a log each time it moves: when it arrived, when each stage started and finished, who reviewed it, whether a person approved it, and which outage stopped it. Everything below is counted from that log while the town runs. Waiting is counted in task-minutes: ten tasks waiting one minute each is ten task-minutes. Eight short lessons walk through this panel.

Little's Law check

A rule from queueing math: on a steady floor, the average number of tasks in the system equals how fast tasks finish times how long each one takes. Times here are fixed, which hides one thing: in real work, where times vary, a desk 86% busy still builds a line.

Quality and security rules

The defect rule and the poisoned-request rule are off at the start, so lessons 1 to 5 read exactly as written. Each follows a fixed rule, not chance, so every run is the same.

A poisoned request carries an instruction hidden in the content an agent reads (called prompt injection), asking the execution agent to misuse a tool. The checkpoint is a policy check that runs before each tool action.

The teaching questions

What depends on the connection, and where does work wait?

The tractor and harvester use local RTK correction to work within the chosen field tolerance. The clinic desk and two agents call a remote model API. Power holds the dock, bike plant, co-op mill and agents; the water tower tracks storage and pressure during a power cut. The model keeps these effects separate: lower water pressure does not change another process's output. The shift label and walking figures mark a handoff; staffing and arrivals stay fixed.

On the agent floor, the AI agents produce tasks faster than a single person can review them. Review becomes the constraint: its queue grows and cycle time stretches while the agents sit partly idle. Adding capacity at the constraint, not elsewhere, is what moves throughput.

Try this sequence

1. Choose each service in Trace to keep its dependents highlighted; the service pills offer a quick hover or keyboard-focus preview. The rings include operational, reporting and context links, so don't assume every highlighted object will stop in an outage. 2. Open Plant: kits pile up in front of paint. Predict what a second paint booth does, then add it and watch the constraint move to assembly. 3. Open Agents and watch the review queue; predict the completed-task cycle time with two reviewers, then press Compare. 4. Cut the internet: field machines keep working and buffer reports; research, planning and the clinic admin desk wait. Restore it and watch reports resend at six a minute. 5. Cut RTK: harvesting stops by policy; an already-dispatched grain load can finish its trip. The harvester generates no new loads until correction returns. 6. Cut power: the dock, plant, co-op and agents stop while the water tower drains slowly from storage.

What is being counted?

Two supplier trucks each bring four crates of parts to the Cycle Works dock: twelve minutes inbound, four minutes of powered unloading. Four crates make one bike kit. The plant uses the original lesson's line: cut and weld 45 minutes on 2 stations, paint 60 on 1, wheels 30 on 2, assembly 40 on 1, so paint caps output at one bike an hour while supply offers about 2.5 kits an hour. The farm's harvester fills a grain cart every 40 working minutes; a farm truck hauls it nine minutes to the co-op, dumps for five powered minutes, and the mill grinds a load in 25. The water tower loses 0.5% of its volume a minute to demand and refills at 1.2% with the pump powered; its pressure is tracked, but downstream water use is not modeled. The clock changes shift labels at 14:00 and 22:00; staffing and arrivals do not change at those handoffs. Agent tasks arrive every four minutes; research 3, planning 3, execution 4 (two slots), review by a person 6, approval by the shift lead 5 for every third task; every fifth task fails review once. The clinic admin desk handles a fictional request every ten minutes. Average cycle time includes finished tasks only; tasks still in progress are excluded. Every number is invented for the lesson and deterministic. Real work varies far more.

What the picture does and does not mean: a reviewer in the model is review capacity with a fixed six-minute service time; the leaning, reaching figures are illustration, not modeled effort or fatigue. The amber blocks are tasks waiting for review, not physical goods. Harbor St closes only after the intake rack (16) and the overflow pad (20) are both full, and it reopens when the queue drops back below that.

All names, plates, manifests and tickets come from a fictional mock-data file. Utility routes are schematic: overhead feeders on shared poles with fiber below, water mains buried along the opposite curb. Road vehicles are visual route followers; the model does not simulate traffic safety, perception, radio propagation, yield or clinical care.

Our interpretation and chosen policies

Losing the RTK correction usually degrades accuracy rather than removing position entirely; here the machine stops because accuracy falls below the field tolerance we chose. Corrections arrive by local radio in this model; many farms receive them over cellular, which would couple an internet outage to positioning. Self-powered vehicles continue through district power loss. Buffered reports resend at six per minute; no retry logic or emergency power is modeled.

Agents at desks make software work visible; their fixed times are a simplification. The clinic queue handles fictional administrative requests only, with no patient records or clinical decisions.
